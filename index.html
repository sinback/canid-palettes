What am I looking at?

Dogs, wolves, coyotes and foxes are dichromats: they have two cone types (about 430 nm and 555 nm) where humans have three. Each result row shows your color on the left and an approximation of how a canid sees it on the right.

The simulation uses Hunt-Pointer-Estevez LMS matrices and collapses the human L and M cones into one canid L response. It is a principled approximation, not a measured transform. Type help to list the commands, or use the mode button above the text to switch models.

The modes

mode canid (default). Canids have two cone types, so this model keeps two color channels, roughly blue and yellow. Reds, oranges and greens shift toward the same yellowish-olive range, while blues stay distinguishable from yellows.

mode rat (experimental). This model keeps a single channel: the rat’s green-sensitive cone. With one cone type there is no hue information, only brightness, so every color reduces to one brightness level. (Rats also have a UV-sensitive cone, but a screen cannot display UV, so it is not modeled.)

Why green rather than gray? The output is drawn in green only because that is the cone being modeled; it is a way to display one channel, not a hue the rat perceives. Black stays black, white becomes full green (#00ff00), and the brighter a color looks to that cone, the brighter its green. Different colors of similar brightness come out the same.
